The refined Kochen–Specker graph

The eighteen rays and nine bases of Cabello, Estebaranz and García-Alcaine in four dimensions, drawn twice: as a Kochen–Specker configuration, and as the finite obstruction at a leaf of Olivia's winning strategy in the model existence game. Both are uncolourable. The second records three things that the first takes for granted. The same comparison is then made for the 49-ray set found by our computer search.

1. Which identifications the contradiction uses

In a Kochen–Specker configuration a ray is a single vertex shared by the bases that contain it, so the hypothesis of noncontextuality is built into the picture. In the obstruction, each ray appears once in each of its two bases, and an explicit identification, derived from axioms that do not mention the measure, says that the two occurrences are one projection.

Every one of the eighteen identifications is needed. Omit any one of them and the system becomes colourable: choose one below to see an assignment with exactly one value 1 in every basis.

2. The same comparison for the 49-ray set of our search

The interactive picture of the 49-ray set found by our computer search shows a Kochen–Specker set in three dimensions: 49 rays and 36 orthogonal bases, uncolourable and ray-critical. The Kochen–Specker theorem says only that this set admits no 0/1 assignment with exactly one value 1 in each basis. Read as an obstruction of the game, the set has 108 occurrences: 16 rays lie in one basis, 9 in two, 22 in three and 2 in four.

Every one of the 92 occurrences of the 33 shared rays is indispensable. Detach any single occurrence from the other occurrences of its ray, and the system becomes colourable; a SAT solver finds the assignment in each of the 92 cases. The Kochen–Specker theorem cannot see this: in a configuration, a ray is one vertex.

3. Two more things the obstruction records

Values with error. The parity argument tolerates error. Suppose each occurrence gets a value in [0, δ] ∪ [1 − δ, 1] and each basis sum lies within η of 1. If 4δ + η < 1, each basis has exactly one high occurrence, so there are nine counted basis by basis and an even number counted ray by ray (for the 49-ray set, whose bases have three rays, the condition is 3δ + η < 1). This estimate applies equally to the Kochen–Specker configuration. What the game adds is the origin of the intervals: they are explicit commitments that Josh makes during the play, and the obstruction records how their refutation is derived.

Where the rays sit. A configuration is fixed in advance. In the game the Hilbert space is infinite-dimensional, and σ-additivity makes Josh commit nearly all the probability to finitely many basis vectors. His later answers single out one of them, and the eighteen rays are built in the block of four basis vectors that contains it: the hypothetical measure itself locates the four-dimensional subspace in which it fails.
